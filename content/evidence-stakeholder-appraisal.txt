# Stakeholder Evidence: Quality Check

## How Representative Is This Evidence?

### Where my evidence came from

Most of my stakeholder evidence comes from:
- **Big technology and media companies** (Amazon, Apple, Google, Microsoft, Washington Post)
- **Large surveys** (Microsoft Work Trend Index, Gallup)
- **News stories** about high-profile conflicts and protests

### Why my employees might be different

**My own employees might react differently than these samples because:**

**Industry and company size differences:** Big tech companies have highly educated, well-paid workers with lots of job options. My organization might employ people with different characteristics (skills that don't transfer easily, lower pay, fewer job choices), which could change how they react to hybrid policies.

**Age and career stage differences:** Younger employees and people early in their careers might value in-person mentoring and networking more than experienced people who already have strong professional networks. The surveys don't always separate out these different groups.

**Commuting differences:** Employees in big cities with good public transportation face different commuting challenges than people in car-dependent suburbs or rural areas. The Apple/Amazon cases involve employees in major cities (San Francisco, Seattle) with decent public transit - my situation might be different.

**Family situation differences:** Employees with young kids, elderly parents to care for, or other family responsibilities might have way stronger preferences for remote work than people without these demands. Survey data often lumps all these groups together.

**Disability and health differences:** Employees who need remote work as a disability accommodation have totally different stakes than employees without disabilities. This group often doesn't get represented well in surveys.

### But the evidence still gives me useful scenarios

Even with these concerns, the stakeholder evidence shows me realistic possibilities:

- **Some employees will like hybrid:** Especially people who feel isolated, miss socializing with coworkers, or want clearer separation between work and home life. The Gallup data on "thriving" shows a good chunk of remote workers are struggling with overall wellbeing.

- **Others will be mad if they organized their lives around being fully remote:** Especially people who moved away, arranged childcare around not commuting, or just really like the independence of remote work. The Amazon/Apple protests prove this isn't just a small fringe group.

- **How many people want what matters a lot:** Even if most people support hybrid, a loud minority fighting against it could still cause turnover problems or hurt morale.

**Bottom line on representativeness: Moderate.** The evidence comes from famous companies and big surveys, which gives me useful information but isn't perfectly matched to my specific organization and employees.

## Who Has Power and What Do They Care About?

### High-power stakeholders (Need to manage these closely)

**Executives, board, major customers:**
- Can force hybrid to happen and set the rules (minimum days required, which jobs are eligible, what happens if you don't comply)
- Control the money needed to make it work (budget for office space, technology, help with commuting costs)
- Set the tone through how they communicate about it

**What they care about:** Company culture, innovation, client relationships, using office space efficiently, being able to see and control what's happening.

**Risk:** If I don't convince them with evidence and a clear business case, they might force through rigid rules that backfire.

### Employees have the power to quit (Need to keep them informed and engaged)

**What the Trip.com & Gallup data show:**

- **If you suddenly take away flexibility people value, some will quit:** The Trip.com experiment showed hybrid cut quitting by 33%, which means forcing everyone back to the office full-time would cause a lot more people to leave. The people who care most about flexibility will find other jobs.

- **If you design hybrid thoughtfully (flexibility, clear reasoning, accommodations), more people will stay:** The BRAC study and Microsoft surveys show that well-designed hybrid can actually make people happier and more likely to stay than full remote.

**What they care about:** Work-life balance, commuting burden, flexibility, independence, chances to advance their careers.

**Risk:** If the rollout feels arbitrary or like they're being forced, I'll lose my best people (who have the most job options) and hurt our reputation for recruiting.

### The power dynamic: Executives can force it, employees can leave

This creates a negotiation zone. The best policy balances what leadership wants (in-person culture) with what employees need (flexibility). Forcing everyone back to the office full-time makes executives happy but people quit. Staying fully remote makes some employees happy but might hurt culture and make leadership nervous.

**Hybrid is the compromise - but only if done well.**

## Overall Confidence in Stakeholder Evidence

**I rate stakeholder evidence as medium quality:**

### What's good about it:

- **Real behavior, not hypothetical:** We have actual protests (Amazon), resignation letters (Apple), survey responses (Gallup, Microsoft), and CEO statements (Jassy). This is what people actually did and said, not just speculation about what might happen.

- **Same patterns show up across multiple sources:** Employees want flexibility but also want connection with coworkers. Leaders care about culture and collaboration. These themes keep appearing across different companies and surveys.

- **Shows what matters for implementation:** The evidence clearly proves that how you roll it out and how you communicate are just as important as the actual hybrid policy for getting good retention and wellbeing. Harsh mandates backfire; collaborative, evidence-based transitions work better.

### What's not so good about it:

- **Not specifically about my organization:** The evidence comes from whatever made the news or got into public surveys, not from a careful sample of my specific employees.

- **News focuses on drama:** News stories emphasize conflict (walkouts, petitions) over people quietly accepting things. We don't know what percentage of Amazon employees actually protested versus just went along with it.

- **No controlled comparisons:** We see resistance to mandates, but we don't see side-by-side comparisons of different ways to implement hybrid at the same company.

- **Don't know how it plays out over time:** How do people's reactions change? Do employees who resist at first eventually adjust, or do they quietly start job hunting and leave months later?

### What this stakeholder evidence IS useful for:

1. **Spotting potential problems:** Which groups of employees are most likely to push back? What are they worried about?
2. **Understanding the implementation challenge:** What communication approaches, accommodations, and flexibility options might reduce resistance?
3. **Having realistic expectations:** No policy will make everyone happy. I need to manage trade-offs, not look for a perfect solution that doesn't exist.

### What this stakeholder evidence is NOT useful for:

1. **Predicting exact retention numbers for my organization:** The surveys and case studies don't give me precise estimates of how many people will quit under different hybrid scenarios.
2. **Deciding whose preferences should win:** Should I prioritize what employees want versus what leadership thinks is best? Evidence can inform this but can't answer it - that's a values question.

## How to Make This Stakeholder Evidence Better

### AI helped me see the gaps and how to fix them:

**Where my stakeholder information is weak:**

I've only used secondary sources (news stories, surveys from other companies). I should do my own internal stakeholder research:

- **Anonymous employee survey:** Find out current satisfaction levels, hybrid preferences, commuting challenges, caregiving responsibilities
- **Focus groups with different employee types:** Compare early-career versus senior people, caregivers versus non-caregivers, long commutes versus short commutes
- **Manager feedback sessions:** What are frontline managers hearing from their teams? What worries do they have about managing hybrid teams?
- **Exit interview analysis:** Are people leaving because they want more or less remote work? What would have kept them?

**Questions I could ask to test how people will react:**

AI suggested these specific questions:

- "What's the minimum number of in-office days you'd accept before you'd start looking for another job?"
- "What would make coming to the office valuable enough that you'd want to come in on your own?"
- "What flexibility or accommodations would you need to make hybrid work for your life situation?"
- "If you left over a hybrid policy, where would you go? Do our competitors offer better flexibility?"

These questions would give me data specific to my organization instead of relying on general surveys and news stories.
