# Scientific Evidence: Quality Appraisal

## Bloom et al. (Trip.com study)

### What's good about this study

**They used random assignment - this is really important**

They randomly decided who got to work hybrid and who had to come in every day. This means we can actually say hybrid CAUSED the lower quit rates and better satisfaction, not just that they happened to go together. 

Without random assignment, maybe the happier employees would have chosen hybrid anyway, and we'd be confused about what caused what. But randomization eliminates that problem.

**Big sample size (1,612 people) and long-term follow-up (18-24 months)**

Having over 1,600 people in the study gives the results statistical power - meaning the findings are less likely to be just random chance. And following people for 18-24 months is great because it shows the benefits lasted. A lot of workplace changes feel good at first but then people get used to them and the effect disappears. This study shows hybrid's benefits stuck around.

**They measured outcomes that actually matter**

They tracked actual quit rates (not just "are you thinking about quitting?"), real performance ratings from managers, and actual promotion decisions. These are concrete outcomes that executives care about, not just survey questions about how people feel.

### Limitations of this study

**It's just one company in China with mostly engineers**

Trip.com is a Chinese travel tech company, and most of the employees in the study were engineers. This might limit how much we can generalize:
- Chinese workplace culture might be different from US/Western companies
- Tech companies might have different needs than other industries
- Engineers might respond differently to hybrid than salespeople, nurses, or creative workers

That said, the basic reasons why hybrid helped (less commuting, better work-life balance) should apply pretty broadly to knowledge workers.

**They only tested one specific hybrid schedule**

Everyone in the hybrid group worked from home on Wednesdays and Fridays. We don't know if it would work the same with:
- Different days (like Monday/Friday)
- More days at home (like 3 instead of 2)
- Letting employees choose which days

They had to pick one schedule for the experiment, but it means we can't be 100% sure other hybrid schedules would work as well.

## Choudhury et al. (BRAC study)

### What's good about this study

**They tested three levels: mostly office, hybrid, and mostly remote**

Unlike the Trip.com study that only compared hybrid to full office, this one included mostly-office, hybrid, AND mostly-remote groups. This is perfect for my situation because I can directly see what happens when you go from mostly remote to hybrid. That's exactly the change I'm planning.

**They measured both performance and wellbeing**

They looked at both "hard" outcomes (manager performance ratings) and "soft" outcomes (employee surveys on satisfaction, work-life balance, isolation). This is important because it shows hybrid didn't just help one thing while hurting another - it improved both productivity AND employee wellbeing at the same time.

### Limitations of this study

**It was done at a non-profit in Bangladesh**

BRAC is a big NGO (non-governmental organization) in Bangladesh, which is pretty different from most American for-profit companies:
- People who work at mission-driven non-profits might have different priorities than corporate employees
- Bangladesh has different infrastructure, commuting issues, and cultural norms than the US
- NGO workers might have different values and preferences

That said, the basic reasons why hybrid worked (better collaboration in-person, avoiding isolation) should apply pretty much everywhere.

**Managers knew which employees were in which group**

If managers knew whether someone was mostly remote, hybrid, or mostly office, they might have unconsciously rated the hybrid employees higher because they expected them to do better. This is called a "halo effect" or "expectancy bias."

So the performance ratings might be a bit inflated for the hybrid group. But the employee surveys (satisfaction, isolation) are less affected by this problem since employees rated themselves.

## Overall: How confident am I in this scientific evidence?

**I'd give it a 4 out of 5 - pretty high confidence**

**Why I'm confident:**
- We have multiple experimental studies (not just surveys) pointing the same way
- The effect sizes are real and meaningful, not tiny
- The findings held up in different countries (China and Bangladesh), different industries (tech and non-profit), and different types of workers
- The reasons why hybrid works make sense (less isolation, better collaboration, good work-life balance)
- Even the BLS (Bureau of Labor Statistics) review articles that summarize lots of studies reach the same conclusions

**Why I'm not giving it a 5/5:**
- We've only studied a limited number of companies and contexts - my company might be different
- Some measurement issues (like managers knowing who was in which group, fixed schedules)
- Most studies only followed people for about 2 years - we don't know if the benefits last longer

**Bottom line:** The evidence is definitely strong enough to justify trying hybrid work, probably as a pilot program first, and then tracking results to make sure we're actually getting the benefits in our specific situation.
