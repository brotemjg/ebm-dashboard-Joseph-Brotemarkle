# Organizational Evidence: Quality Check

## Using Module 9's 10 Barriers Framework to Evaluate Organizational Data

### 1. Sampling / How representative is it?

**Assessment: Low risk / Highly representative**

The BLS, Census, and USAFacts data cover the entire U.S. workforce using big, carefully designed samples. The Current Population Survey asks about 60,000 households every month, and the American Community Survey reaches millions of people each year. These aren't just random online polls or people who chose to respond - they use proper scientific sampling to make sure they represent everyone.

**What this means for my decision:**

These data represent the whole national labor market, which is broader than just my organization, but they're super credible for understanding what's normal. If my workforce is similar to the typical American knowledge workers, these numbers apply directly to me. If my organization is unusual in some way (like we're all in one weird niche industry), I might need to adjust my thinking a bit.

**Bottom line: Representativeness is a major strength here.**

### 2. Measurement / How accurately is it measured?

**Assessment: Low to moderate risk / Reasonably good quality**

"Usually works at home" and "did some work at home" come from what people report in surveys. This means there could be some errors (people might not remember exactly or might misunderstand the question), but these are standardized questions asked the same way every year, so comparing trends over time is valid.

The BLS uses clear definitions:
- "Did some work at home" = worked at least a few hours at home on days they worked
- "Usually worked from home" = home is the main work location

**Limitations:** 

Surveys where people report their own behavior can be less accurate than actual records (like security badge swipes showing who came to the office), but for work location, self-reporting is pretty reliable - people generally know where they work.

**Bottom line: Good enough quality for understanding how common remote work is and how it's changing.**

### 3. Causality / Does this prove cause and effect?

**Assessment: High risk / This is a major limitation**

**This is the biggest weakness of organizational data for my decision.**

National data tell me what's happening, but not why. They can tell me:
- How common remote/hybrid work is
- How these numbers have changed over time
- Correlations (like "job postings with 'remote' in them get more applicants")

They do NOT prove that:
- Hybrid causes better retention (it could be backwards: happy companies offer hybrid, not hybrid makes companies happy)
- Offering hybrid will definitely cause my desired outcomes

**To prove cause and effect, I need the experimental studies (like Bloom and Choudhury), not just the organizational data.**

These national numbers are perfect for showing what's normal and what the trends are, but they're weak for predicting what will happen if I make a change.

### 4. Context / Does this apply to my situation?

**Assessment: Moderate risk**

**National averages might not perfectly match what's happening in my specific situation.**

If my organization is in a heavily regulated industry (like healthcare or financial services), or a business where we need to see clients in person, the national averages might make remote work look more common than it really is among my actual competitors.

On the flip side, if I'm in tech or a creative industry, the national averages might make remote work look less common than it really is in my peer group.

**How to handle this:** 

I should add industry-specific or competitor-specific data when I can. I can check job postings from competitors, look at industry surveys, or read trade association reports to see if hybrid is standard in my specific sector.

### 5. Timeliness / Is this data recent and relevant?

**Assessment: Low risk / Very current**

All the numbers I'm using are from 2023–2025, so they're very up-to-date for my current decision. The data capture what's happening after the pandemic has settled down (not just the chaos period), so they show the "new normal" instead of temporary emergency adjustments.

**This is a strength: the data is fresh and relevant.**

### 6-10. Other quality issues (less relevant for this type of data)

**Publication bias:** Not a problem here - these are government statistics that report everything they measure, not cherry-picked results.

**Conflicts of interest:** Low risk - BLS and Census don't have any financial reason to make remote work look more or less common than it really is.

**Transparency:** High - the methods and data are all publicly available and anyone can check them.

**Precision:** Moderate - the national estimates are pretty precise because they survey so many people, but when you break it down by specific occupations, the estimates get less precise.

**Relevance:** High - the data directly answer the question "how common is hybrid/remote work?"

---

## Overall Confidence

I'd rate organizational data as **high quality for showing context and what's normal**, but **weak for proving cause and effect or predicting my specific outcomes**.

**This data is perfect for:**

1. Showing how common hybrid is in the job market (proving I'm not making a weird or risky choice)
2. Arguing that hybrid helps with recruitment and retention because it matches what workers expect now
3. Proving that remote/hybrid work is a permanent shift, not just a temporary pandemic thing

**This data is NOT enough for:**

1. Proving that hybrid will definitely cause better retention or performance in my specific organization
2. Estimating how much benefit I'll actually see (for that, I need the experimental studies)
3. Predicting exactly how my employees will react (for that, I need to talk to stakeholders)

**How to use it strategically:** Combine organizational data (for context) + scientific studies (for proof of cause and effect) + stakeholder input (for implementation details) to build a complete argument.
