# Practitioner Evidence: Credibility & Relevance Assessment

## How credible are these sources?

### McKinsey, Gallup, and Microsoft are pretty trustworthy

These organizations use large samples and are transparent about their methods. They're not academic researchers, but they're high-quality practitioner sources that are generally reliable for understanding what's happening in the business world and how employees are feeling.

### The news coverage is credible but has some limits

The news articles from outlets like The Verge, The Guardian, and Business Insider are credible and well-sourced. But here's the thing - they tend to focus on the most dramatic stuff (protests, walkouts, angry internal letters) rather than showing the full picture of how employees react. The extreme reactions get covered, but we don't hear as much about the employees who just accepted the change and moved on.

---

## Potential biases and limitations

### These consulting and tech companies have skin in the game

McKinsey and Microsoft both benefit from promoting hybrid work:
- McKinsey sells consulting services to help companies transform their workplace strategies
- Microsoft sells Teams, Office 365, and other tools that are marketed as hybrid work solutions

So they might have a bias toward saying "hybrid is necessary and you can make it work (if you hire us or buy our products)." They're not necessarily wrong, but we should keep their business interests in mind.

### News articles focus on drama, not normalcy

News outlets cover protests and conflicts because that's what gets clicks. They might be making employee discontent look bigger than it really is. The truth is, walkouts and petitions make better headlines than "most employees adapted fine." So we're probably seeing an exaggerated view of how badly return-to-office policies can go.

### None of this proves cause and effect

These practitioner sources show correlations and tell stories, but they don't prove that hybrid CAUSES better retention or that remote work CAUSES isolation. They're based on surveys, interviews, and observations, which can only show associations, not definitive cause-and-effect relationships. That's what the scientific studies are for.

---

## Overall judgment

**I'd rate this practitioner evidence as moderate quality.**

It's really useful for understanding how employees and leaders actually experience these changes and what implementation challenges I might face. But I can't rely on this alone to predict how big the impact will be. I need to combine this with the scientific studies and organizational data to make a strong case.
