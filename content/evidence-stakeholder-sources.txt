# Stakeholder Evidence: What Different Groups Think

## What Different Stakeholders Say About Hybrid vs Fully Remote

### Employees (remote-capable knowledge workers)

#### Microsoft Work Trend Index surveys:

Over 70% of workers want to keep flexible remote options, AND over 65% also want more in-person time with their teams.

**What this means:** Employees want hybrid with flexibility, not all-office or all-remote. This is the key tension: most people don't want to lose remote work entirely, but they also miss seeing their teammates in person. The sweet spot is a mix that gives them autonomy while still allowing collaboration.

#### Gallup surveys on engagement and wellbeing:

Fully remote workers can be highly engaged (motivated and focused on work) but are the least likely to say they're "thriving" overall. They're more likely to feel negative emotions (loneliness, anger, sadness) than people working hybrid.

**What this means:** Being engaged at work isn't the same as being happy overall. You can be productive and focused but still feel isolated and unhappy. Hybrid seems to support both job engagement AND overall wellbeing, while full remote might support engagement but hurt wellbeing.

#### When hybrid is forced on people as a rigid mandate (real examples):

Employees sometimes protest or even quit. Here are some examples:

- **Amazon:** Thousands of employees walked out to protest the 3-day return-to-office mandate. Internal Slack channels and petitions argued the policy was arbitrary, not based on evidence, and would hurt diversity by forcing out parents and people who had relocated.

- **Apple:** Employees wrote internal letters and organized resistance to Apple's return-to-office policy, saying they had already proven they could work well remotely and deserved more flexibility.

- **Google:** Employees complained that performance reviews and promotions were being influenced by whether they showed up at the office, creating a "two-tier" system where remote workers were at a disadvantage.

**What this means:** How you roll out a policy matters just as much as what the policy is. Employees push back when:
- Mandates feel arbitrary or dictatorial (no one asked for their input, no clear reason given)
- There's no flexibility for different life situations (caring for kids or elderly parents, having a disability, having moved away)
- The policy seems to be about control or watching people rather than actual business needs

### Senior leadership / executives

#### Amazon's CEO Andy Jassy and other CEOs say:

In-person work makes culture stronger, improves collaboration, and speeds up decision-making. That's why Amazon, Disney, and other big companies have pushed for 3- or 5-day office mandates.

**Why leadership thinks this way:** Executives often believe:
- Culture gets built through informal interactions (hallway chats, lunch together, after-work drinks) that don't happen on Zoom
- Innovation happens through random collisions and spontaneous brainstorming, which are harder to do remotely
- Mentoring and teaching junior employees requires watching and coaching them in person
- Decisions get made faster when people can just walk into each other's offices

**Important note:** Many of these beliefs come from gut feeling and pre-pandemic experience, not from actual studies. Some executives ignore research evidence (like Bloom's Trip.com study) because they think their company or industry is "different."

#### Many leaders think innovation and mentoring work better in person, even when productivity data says remote is fine.

This shows a gap: even when productivity numbers show remote work doesn't hurt performance (or even helps it), leaders still worry about things that are harder to measure - like innovation, culture, and developing employees long-term.

**What this means for me:** To convince senior leadership, I might need to address these softer concerns, not just show them productivity and retention numbers.

### Customers / clients & local communities

#### McKinsey's research on commercial real estate and downtown economies:

When people stopped commuting to downtown offices, it hurt downtown businesses - restaurants, retail shops, and service businesses all saw less foot traffic. Public transit lost riders too. Hybrid work (2-3 days in the office) can bring back some foot traffic without making everyone come back full-time.

**What this means:** Work arrangement decisions have ripple effects. Local communities got hurt economically by remote work. While this might not be my main concern, it's worth knowing that hybrid policies affect more than just my organization.

#### For some businesses with clients, in-person meetings still matter:

In consulting, financial services, healthcare, and other industries where you work directly with clients, those clients might prefer or even require in-person meetings for building relationships, discussing confidential stuff, or solving complex problems.

**What this means:** If my business involves a lot of client interaction, hybrid (which keeps some in-person availability) might be more appealing to clients than being fully remote. This could be another business reason for going hybrid, beyond just what employees prefer.

## Practical Impact: Who Wins and Who Loses

### Moving from fully remote to hybrid helps powerful stakeholders:

- **Leaders:** Feel better about culture, collaboration, and being able to see what's happening. They get reassurance that teams are actually working and building relationships.
- **Some clients:** Might appreciate easier access to in-person meetings and relationship-building.
- **Local businesses:** Get more customers on days when employees come to the office.

### Can help employees' wellbeing if done right:

- **Less isolation:** Employees get regular face-to-face contact, which fights loneliness.
- **Sense of belonging:** Being physically present makes team bonds stronger and helps people feel more connected to the organization.
- **Keeps flexibility:** If hybrid still means 2-3 days at home, employees keep most of the work-life balance benefits of remote work.

### Risks major backlash if done badly:

- **If required days are inflexible:** Employees with kids, disabilities, or long commutes might feel forced to choose between their job and their life circumstances.
- **If commuting is expensive:** Employees who moved farther away during the pandemic, or who have expensive/long commutes, might see hybrid as basically a pay cut (more time and money spent on commuting).
- **If the reason given is weak:** Employees fight back against mandates that feel like "because I said so" instead of being based on real business needs and evidence.

**Key lesson from the big tech companies:** Amazon, Apple, and Google all got employee resistance - not because people hated in-person work completely, but because the policies felt harsh, inflexible, and ignored all the evidence showing remote work had actually worked well during the pandemic.

## Ethical Impact: Fairness and Inclusion Issues

### How hybrid can be good for fairness:

- **Less loneliness and isolation:** This especially matters for people early in their careers, introverts who have trouble getting noticed on Zoom, and people who don't have good home office setups.
- **Better mentoring for junior employees:** Younger workers benefit way more from watching experienced people work, getting feedback in person, and networking face-to-face. Being fully remote can put them at a disadvantage.
- **More equal shot at promotions:** If getting ahead requires being visible and building relationships with leaders, fully remote workers might get overlooked ("out of sight, out of mind"). Hybrid makes sure everyone gets some face time.

### How hybrid can create unfairness:

- **Commuting hits certain groups harder:**
  - **Caregivers (mostly women):** Might have organized their schedules around picking up kids from school, taking care of elderly parents, etc. Adding commute days messes up these carefully balanced arrangements.
  - **Disabled employees:** Might have been using remote work as a disability accommodation. Requiring office presence could be discriminatory if not done carefully.
  - **Employees who moved away:** During full remote, some people moved farther from the office (to cheaper areas, to be near family, etc.). Making them come back basically punishes them for something they were allowed to do.
  - **Lower-income employees:** Commuting costs (gas, parking, public transit, work clothes, eating out) hurt lower-paid people more.

- **If it's "come in or quit," that creates unfair pressure:** Employees who were hired as remote, or who made major life decisions based on remote work being permanent, will feel betrayed. This damages trust and makes the company look bad.

**How to avoid these problems:** Offer accommodations, phase it in slowly, help pay for commuting, let people pick which days they come in, focus on results instead of just counting who's in the office.
